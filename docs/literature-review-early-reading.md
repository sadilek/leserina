# Picture-word matching and early reading: a literature review

Can a picture-word matching game like Leserina teach children their first words? From what age? Can
it get in the way of learning to read and spell later? This review draws on about 20 studies and
reviews, linked at the end. Written in October 2026.

## In short

**Can children learn to recognise words this way?** Yes, a small set of words. Matching printed words
to pictures is a well-studied way to teach a first "sight vocabulary". It works for preschoolers who
are typically developing and for those with Down syndrome. But a child who can do it is recognising word
shapes and single letters. That is a precursor of reading, not reading itself. Real word reading starts
once the child connects letters with sounds, and matching alone does not teach that.

**From what age?** Not as a baby: a randomised trial at 9–18 months found no effect. From about 2½–3
years, with an adult beside them, children slowly learn their first words by sight (about 15 words in
6 months in one study of 2–4-year-olds). Around 4–6 years, as letter names and sounds come in, they move
from guessing by shape to using letters. In this stage a game like Leserina can do the most.

**Can it hurt later reading and spelling?** No study shows harm from early word play like this, as long
as it adds to learning letters and sounds and does not replace it. Two weak spots: children solve such
tasks with shortcuts (first letter, word length, elimination) and may learn that guessing works, and
pictures can draw attention away from the word. Whole-word learning is not a good *method* for learning
to read (that is the clearest finding of the "reading wars"), but nothing points to harm as an early
extra in German, where school teaches letter–sound rules from the first day.

## 1. What the game trains, in research terms

Leserina asks the child to pair a printed word with its picture. Tapping the word plays it aloud.
Behaviour analysts call this **matching-to-sample**, and it has a long history in reading research.
Sidman (1971) taught a boy who could not read to match *spoken* words to *printed* words. Afterwards he
could also match printed words to pictures and read them aloud, without being taught either. Teaching
two links produced a third:

    spoken word "Kuh"  ⟷  picture of a cow  ⟷  printed word Kuh

Leserina trains picture ⟷ print, with the spoken word as help.

Developmental psychologists describe the same skill with Linnea Ehri's phases of word reading. In the
first phase (**pre-alphabetic**) children recognise words by a striking visual feature, such as a tall
letter or the length. In the **partial alphabetic** phase they use a few letters, usually the first,
together with their sounds. Only in the **full alphabetic** phase do they decode every letter, and only
from there do words go into memory reliably (Ehri calls this *orthographic mapping*). A matching game
can be played in every phase. Which phase the child is in decides what they learn from it.

## 2. Evidence on age

| Age studied | Finding |
|---|---|
| 9–18 months | Baby-reading media: no effect |
| 2–4 years | About 15 sight words in 6 months, taught by a parent |
| 3–5 years | Children "read" logos, but not the words in them |
| about 5 years | Prereaders learn words by visual cues, not sounds |
| 4–7 years | Letter sounds, then decoding |

The ages are those studied, not hard limits. Individual children vary a lot.

### Babies: no

Neuman et al. (2014) gave 117 infants aged 9–18 months a "Your Baby Can Read"-style package (DVDs, word
and picture flashcards, word books) daily for 7 months. On 13 of 14 measures, including eye-tracking
tests, they did no better than the control group. Parents nevertheless believed strongly that it worked.
Children that young seem to respond to the routine and the parent, not to the print.

### Toddlers and young preschoolers: a small sight vocabulary, with an adult

The strongest evidence for this exact game type comes from Down syndrome research. Sue Buckley's group
has taught 2–4-year-olds sight words for decades with a *match → select → name* sequence. That is
Leserina's mechanic plus naming. In Appleton, Buckley & MacDonald (2002), parents taught 18 children with
Down syndrome and 18 typically developing children of the same age (2–4 years, mean 4;1). After six
months the typical children knew about 15 words and the children with Down syndrome about 17. So typical
preschoolers can do it, at a modest rate, with a parent involved.

### The catch: at that age "recognising" means guessing by shape

Masonheimer, Drum & Ehri (1984): of 3–5-year-olds who "read" logos such as PEPSI, almost all failed once
the same word was printed plainly, and most did not notice when letters were swapped (XEXSI). Ehri &
Wilce (1985): kindergarten prereaders learned arbitrary, visually distinctive spellings (like *WcB* for
"elephant") *more easily* than spellings that matched the sounds. Children who knew a few letter sounds
showed the reverse. Gough's thumbprint study makes the same point: children learned words faster from
flashcards carrying a thumbprint, and many then "read" the thumbprint alone. Children grab the single
easiest cue that tells the cards apart.

### The shift that matters comes from letter–sound knowledge

Ehri (2014) sums up the research: lasting sight words need phonemic awareness and letter–sound
knowledge. Words learned by shape alone are fragile and get confused as the set grows, because many
words look alike. In German this shift comes early and easily. Wimmer & Hummer (1990) found that German
first graders hardly used a "logographic" (whole-shape) strategy at all, because the spelling is regular
and school teaches letter sounds from the start. Early shape-reading does not seem to be a step children
must pass through, nor an obstacle to leaving it.

## 3. Could it be counterproductive?

| Concern | What research shows | Risk for Leserina |
|---|---|---|
| **Learning to guess** instead of looking at letters | With four cards a child can win by the first letter, word length or elimination (Gough; Ehri). That is normal for the phase and not harmful in itself. It only becomes a problem if the child gets used to guessing and keeps doing it. That habit is a known feature of weak readers, encouraged by the "three-cueing" methods that reviews now reject (Castles, Rastle & Nation 2018). | **Moderate.** Leserina picks the four words without regard to how similar they look. |
| **Pictures distract** from the print | A classic finding (Samuels 1967; Singh & Solman 1990; Torcasio & Sweller 2010): a picture *on the word card* lets the child name the word without looking at the letters, so fewer words are learned. Preschoolers look at the print of picture books for only about 5 seconds per book (Evans & Saint-Aubin 2005). The effect goes away when the picture comes *after* the attempt, as feedback. | **Low.** In Leserina the word card has no picture: the child has to look at the word to find its partner, and the picture works as the answer. |
| **Whole-word instead of phonics** | As a *teaching method*, whole-word learning loses clearly to systematic phonics. In Taylor, Davis & Rastle (2017), adults learning an invented script with print-to-meaning training (the Leserina principle) did worse at reading aloud and transfer to new words, and even at understanding, than with print-to-sound training. De Rose et al. (1996) found matching games led to reading new words mainly when children also built the words from letters. | **Low as an extra, high if it replaced letter learning.** Leserina is a home game next to school phonics, not instead of it. |
| **Spelling** | No study found that seeing correctly spelled words early harms spelling. The risk lies elsewhere: spelling memory builds on letter–sound mapping (Ehri 2014), so a whole-word game adds little to spelling. Seeing the correct spelling over and over is, if anything, helpful. | **Low.** Leserina shows only correct spellings. |
| **Starting early** at all | No harm found, but no lasting head start either. Children taught to read at 5 had no advantage by age 11 over those taught at 7, and in a second sample the late starters understood text slightly better (Suggate, Schaughency & Reese 2013; review: Suggate 2013, from Steiner-school research). Swiss children who already read at school entry stayed ahead for eight years (Stamm, 1995–2008), but they had mostly taught themselves, a self-selected group, so this says little about teaching. | **Low.** Fine as play, not worth pushing. |
| **Screen time** and pressure | WHO (2019): no more than 1 hour of sedentary screen time a day for 2–4-year-olds, less is better. Touch-screen word learning works at 2–4 years, and 4-year-olds transfer it to real objects best; dragging helped some groups more than tapping (Russo-Johnson et al. 2017). The baby-media trial shows how easily parents overrate what a child "reads". | **Low.** Rounds are short, and a drag is the main action. |

## 4. What follows for Leserina

The game is on the right side of the research in several ways. The word card has no picture, the
picture works as feedback, and the word list is ordered by how easy each word is to *decode*, so it fits
letter–sound learning. The spaced repetition does not count a word as learned when the child had it read
aloud first. The open points are guessing and the missing letter–sound link:

- **Make guessing harder as the child gets better**: from some level on, put words with the same first
  letter or the same length into one round (Maus / Mama, Hose / Hase), so the child has to look past the
  first letter.
- **Draw attention to letters and sounds**: for example, read a word aloud sound by sound ("M–a–u–s") on
  a long press, or offer a mode that builds the word from letters. That would move the game from
  shape-reading to the phase that lasts (Ehri; de Rose et al.).
- **For parents**: sit next to the child at 3–4 years and keep sessions short. Do not read success in
  the game as reading; check by showing a word without the four-card choice.
